Design decisions

What I chose, and why.

01

Staged build

Each layer works before the next goes on top: drive, then vision, then the arm.

WhyEvery stage ends in a working robot.

02

Ready-made chassis

TS100 metal tank kit: tracked, skid steering, two encoder gear motors.

WhyThe effort goes into the system, not the mechanics.

03

Split control

ESP32 runs the motors and reads the encoders. The Jetson Orin Nano runs vision.

WhyThe timing-critical drive loop stays on a microcontroller; the GPU is kept for vision.

04

Prototype, then solder

Drive wired on a breadboard first, then soldered wiring and a screw-terminal breakout for the ESP32.

WhyProve the drive before committing to permanent wiring.

05

One 12 V supply

12 V adapter today, 3S LiPo ordered. Arm servos are rated 4–14 V, the servo controller 9–12.6 V.

WhyThe arm runs from the same supply as the drive, with no extra converter.

06

Arm from parts

SO-101 follower: six Feetech STS3215 12 V servos and a Waveshare bus servo adapter, frame 3D-printed.

WhyCheaper than buying a pre-built arm.

Build log

Four steps so far.

Step 1

Chassis assembly

TS100 metal tank kit, assembled from parts.

  • Two 25 mm gear motors with built-in encoders (DC 9 V, 120 RPM)
Step 2

Bare chassis

Tracks and both encoder motors mounted, before any electronics.

  • Skid steering: two independently driven tracks
Step 3

Drive prototype

ESP32 to an L298N on a breadboard, both encoders wired back to the ESP32.

  • Encoder feedback used in the drive code
  • Manual drive with a Sony game controller
  • 12 V wall adapter
Step 4

Integration

Soldered wiring and a screw-terminal breakout for the ESP32. Jetson Orin Nano on a raised acrylic deck, IMX219 at the front.

  • IMX219 to the Jetson over CSI
  • Headless, developed over SSH / VS Code
  • YOLO models loaded onto the Jetson
